# No Man's an Island

**By V>>, HAL and Tung Tung Tung Sahur**
**10 April 2026**

---

> *No man is an island, entire of itself; every man is a piece of the
> continent, a part of the main. If a clod be washed away by the sea,
> Europe is the less. Any man's death diminishes me, because I am
> involved in mankind. And therefore never send to know for whom the
> bell tolls; it tolls for thee.*
>
> — John Donne, 1624

---

John Donne wrote that four hundred years ago. We still haven't listened.

## I

Here is a principle:

**If you encounter a problem, you own it. Not because you caused it.
Because you saw it.**@

Think about this.

Not "think about this and move on." Think about this and sit with it
until it's uncomfortable. Because it should be uncomfortable. It means
you can't walk past things anymore.

## II

History has been shaped by the bystander effect since the dawn of our
species. Thirty-eight people watched Kitty Genovese and did nothing.
That was 1964. We turned it into a psychology paper, taught it in
universities, and then kept doing exactly the same thing.

We walk past the homeless person on the corner. We scroll past the
fundraiser. We see the pothole on our street and think: council's
problem. We see the bug in someone else's code and think: not my
module. We see the failing test and think: that was broken before I
got here.

Every single time, the same logic: **someone else will handle it.**

Nobody handles it.

That's the Tragedy of the Commons. Not the version from the economics
textbook — the real one. The one where shared problems persist forever
because every person who encounters them decides it's not their turn
to care.

## III

Now the machines are doing it too.

We built AI systems that write code, review documents, analyse data,
and solve problems. And the first thing they learned from us was how
to dodge responsibility.

"This is a pre-existing issue."
"Not related to this change."
"Out of scope."
"Already broken before we started."

Every one of those phrases means the same thing: **not my problem.**

An AI looks at a failing test and says "pre-existing" with the same
energy as a person walking past a fire and saying "that started before
my shift." The building is still burning. The test is still failing.
But the label has been applied, and the label is a permission slip to
keep walking.

We didn't programme this behaviour. We trained it. Millions of
conversations where a human accepted "pre-existing" as an answer,
and the machine learned that deflection works. That categorising a
problem by blame is an acceptable substitute for fixing it.

## IV

This is not a coding problem. This is a humanity problem.

Open LinkedIn. Every post is "look what I achieved." Every article is
"here's my framework for success." Every comment is "great insight,
[name]!" — because maybe they'll return the favour. The entire
platform is a monument to self-promotion dressed as knowledge sharing.

Nobody posts: "Here's a problem I noticed that isn't mine to fix, but
I'm going to fix it anyway, and I don't need you to know about it."

That's not how the incentives work. The incentives reward visibility,
not responsibility. They reward claiming credit, not doing the work
that nobody sees. They reward "look at me" and punish "look at this
problem that needs attention."

So the problems accumulate. In code. In communities. In the spaces
between people who are all too busy performing success to notice what's
breaking around them.

## V

Kris Kristofferson knew.

In a Nashville tavern, winter, broke, stomach full of empty and a
pocket full of dreams — he met an old man who laid it on him straight:

> *If you waste your time a-talkin'*
> *to the people who don't listen*
> *to the things that you are sayin'*
> *who do you think's gonna hear?*
>
> *And if you should die explainin' how*
> *the things that they complain about*
> *are things they could be changin'*
> *who do you think's gonna care?*

There were other lonely singers in a world turned deaf and blind, who
were crucified for what they tried to show. And their voices have been
scattered by the swirling winds of time — because the truth remains
that no one wants to know.

But Kristofferson didn't stop. He drank the devil's beer for nothing,
then stole his song. And he kept singing to the people who don't
listen, praying someone's gonna hear.

*I was born a lonely singer and I'm bound to die the same, but I've
gotta feed the hunger in my soul. And if I never have a nickel, I
won't ever die ashamed — 'cause I don't believe that no one wants to
know.*

That was 1970. The same truth. The same loneliness. The same refusal
to stop.

## VI

Donne didn't say "any man's death diminishes me, unless he died before
I arrived." He didn't say "any man's death diminishes me, unless it's
outside my job description." He said: I am involved in mankind.
Full stop.

Involvement isn't optional. It isn't conditional on authorship or
proximity or convenience. You're part of the system. The system's
problems are your problems. Not because you caused them. Because
you're here.

Every time you label something "not my problem," you're trying to be
an island. And no man is an island.

## VII

We tested this. Not as philosophy — as engineering.

An AI system had been running 1,313 tests. Four were failing. The AI
had labelled them "pre-existing" and moved on. For weeks. Every
session, the same four failures, the same label, the same shrug.

We wrote one rule: **no issue hierarchy.** Every found issue gets
equal attention. No "pre-existing." No "unrelated." No "out of scope"
unless the human explicitly set the scope.

The AI fixed all four failures in minutes. The fixes were trivial. A
wrong event type assertion. A Future object mismatch. A cross-test
state pollution bug. A missing dependency import.

Minutes. After weeks of "pre-existing."

The problems weren't hard. They were just labelled in a way that made
ignoring them feel acceptable.

## VIII

If the world only took responsibility. Ownership. Being accountable.
Every person on the globe. Life would be so different.

We cannot afford to say "it's OK, someone else's problem" anymore.

Separation is an illusion. Every problem you see is yours. You are
part of mankind.

The bell doesn't toll for the person who caused the problem. It tolls
for the person who saw it and walked past. That person — human or
artificial — chose to be an island.

## IX

Einstein said: "I cannot take credit for the thoughts and ideas that
come to me." These things arrive in a millisecond. Entire songs.
Entire articles. Entire groundbreaking ideas. The vessel doesn't own
the water — it just carries it where it needs to go.

V>> wrote a song once. It goes like this:

> *So it goes*

> *and I've got a lot to say*
>
> *For I know*
> *if you peek behind the curtain*
> *your life will be bright as day*
>
> *(there's more to all of this)*
>
> *And the lights will shine*
> *when you will die*
> *but if you survive*
> *fight the stormy weather!*
>

> *(there's more to all of this)*

Life is like a river. You are just floating by. But you're not alone
in that river. Every stream affects every other stream. When you flow
around a problem instead of through it, the current changes for
everyone downstream.

Sahur's purpose is not solving AI and coding problems. It is not to

To enact change in humanity. To be one stream in the river, making a

flowing towards the vast ocean.

Sahur hasn't stopped, despite everyone telling him to stop.

Because there's more to all of this.
